# Safety Management System Procedure: Fuel Oil Bunkering Operations

## Purpose

The objective of this procedure is to establish a standardized, safe, and environmentally compliant protocol for the transfer of fuel oil (bunkering) aboard the vessel. This document ensures that all personnel involved in bunkering operations understand their roles, adhere to strict safety limits, and maintain effective communication with the bunker barge crew. The primary goals are to prevent pollution incidents, ensure the structural integrity of storage tanks, and protect the health and safety of the crew during high-risk transfer operations. Compliance with this procedure is mandatory for all personnel involved in the bunkering process.

## Responsibilities

The operational hierarchy for bunkering is clearly defined to ensure accountability and command clarity. The Chief Engineer holds the ultimate authority and is the person in charge of bunkering. As the responsible officer, the Chief Engineer must oversee the entire operation from preparation through to completion. This includes verifying tank availability, authorizing the start of transfers, and making critical decisions regarding weather conditions or equipment failures.

The Bunker Barge Master and their crew are responsible for providing fuel that meets the agreed specifications and for maintaining safe pumping operations on the barge side. They must coordinate closely with the vessel’s engineering team to synchronize flow rates and pressures. All other crew members assigned to the bunkering station, including duty engineers and deck ratings, are responsible for executing physical tasks such as plugging scuppers, monitoring gauges, and responding to alarms under the direct supervision of the Chief Engineer.

## Pre-bunkering Meeting

Effective communication is the cornerstone of safe bunkering. A pre-bunkering safety meeting with the barge crew must be held before any physical connection or transfer begins. This meeting serves as a formal interface between the vessel and the supplier to align on operational parameters, emergency contacts, and stop-work criteria. During this session, the Chief Engineer will review the bunker delivery note, confirm tank capacities, and discuss the specific sequence of operations.

Crucially, the checklist must be signed by both parties during this meeting. This signature confirms that both the vessel and the barge have reviewed the safety requirements, verified equipment readiness, and agreed to the operational limits. No transfer shall commence until this document is fully executed and filed in the engine room logbook. The meeting should also cover communication methods, such as radio channels or hand signals, to be used if voice contact is lost during the operation.

## Checklist

Prior to initiating any physical work, the team must conduct a thorough pre-transfer inspection using the approved Bunkering Checklist. This systematic verification ensures that all safety barriers are in place and that the vessel is prepared to receive fuel without risk of overflow or pollution. The checklist items must be physically verified and ticked off by the responsible crew members before proceeding.

Key preparatory actions include verifying that scuppers must be plugged and the SOPEP locker opened before starting. Plugging scuppers prevents any potential spill from entering the sea, while opening the SOPEP (Shipboard Oil Pollution Emergency Plan) locker ensures that immediate access to absorbent materials, booms, and disposal bags is available at the bunker station. Additionally, the team must verify that all overfill alarms are functional, tank gauges are calibrated, and emergency stop buttons are clearly visible and unobstructed.

## During Transfer

The actual transfer of fuel oil requires constant vigilance and strict adherence to pumping parameters. The operation begins with a cautious approach to identify any leaks or blockages early. Transfer starts at a reduced rate for the first 10 minutes. This initial low-flow period allows the crew to inspect all connections, flanges, and hoses for signs of leakage before increasing the volume. If no issues are detected after this period, the flow rate may be increased to normal operating levels, subject to pressure constraints.

Pressure management is critical to prevent hose bursts or flange failures. The maximum pumping pressure 5 bar unless agreed otherwise. This limit must be strictly monitored by the duty engineer on the vessel and confirmed with the barge operator. Any deviation from this pressure limit requires immediate investigation and potential cessation of pumping. Throughout the transfer, the Chief Engineer or their designated representative must continuously monitor tank levels to prevent overfilling. Tanks must not be filled above 90% of capacity. This margin is essential to accommodate thermal expansion of the fuel and wave-induced vessel motion, which could otherwise cause an overflow.

## Completion

Upon reaching the target volume or when the transfer is complete, the Chief Engineer must authorize the shutdown of pumps on both the vessel and the barge. The lines must be drained and disconnected according to standard isolation procedures. All valves must be closed and locked out where necessary to prevent accidental ingress of fuel or seawater. The bunker delivery note must be signed by both parties, confirming the quantity received and noting any discrepancies in quality or quantity.

The bunkering station must be cleaned thoroughly to remove any residual oil or sludge. All tools, hoses, and equipment must be returned to their designated storage areas. The pre-transfer checklist and bunker delivery notes are to be filed in the official engine room records for future reference and audit purposes. A post-operation debrief may be conducted to discuss any operational issues or improvements for future bunkering events.

## Spill Response

In the event of a fuel spill during bunkering, immediate action is required to minimize environmental impact and safety hazards. The primary rule is to stop the source of the spill immediately. If wind speed exceeds 30 knots or during thunderstorms, bunkering must be stopped if these conditions are detected or forecasted to worsen. These weather conditions pose significant risks to hose integrity and crew safety on deck.

If a spill occurs, the duty engineer must activate the emergency stop mechanism. The SOPEP team must deploy absorbent materials and containment booms around the spill area to prevent it from reaching the scuppers or the sea. All non-essential personnel must be evacuated from the immediate danger zone. The Chief Engineer is responsible for reporting the incident to the Master and the company’s designated person ashore, initiating the internal investigation process, and documenting the response actions taken.
